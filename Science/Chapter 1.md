# Chapter 1.1
**Particulate Nature of Matter**
- Matter consists of microscopic particles called atoms.
    
- Scanning tunneling microscopes (STMs) can image individual atoms, such as silicon atoms with a radius of 117 picometers.
    
- A particulate description focuses on atomic or molecular composition (for example, ozone consisting of three bonded oxygen atoms), whereas macroscopic descriptions focus on observable physical properties like smell, state, or toxicity.
    

**Classification of Matter**

- **Pure Substances**: Cannot be separated by physical processes. They divide into **elements** (cannot be decomposed chemically, like gold or silicon) and **compounds** (can be decomposed chemically, like water).
    
- **Mixtures**: Can be separated by physical processes. They divide into **homogeneous** (uniform composition, like vinegar) and **heterogeneous** (non-uniform composition, like salad dressing).
    

**Scientific Methods**

- **Hypothesis**: A tentative, testable explanation for an observed natural phenomenon.
    
- **Experiment**: A controlled test designed to evaluate a hypothesis by isolating variables.
    
- **Scientific Law**: A concise description of consistent, fundamental relationships in nature.
    
- **Scientific Theory**: A well-tested, comprehensive explanation of _why_ natural phenomena occur.
    

**Foundational Chemical Laws**

- **Law of Definite Proportions (Constant Composition)**: A specific compound always contains the exact same proportions of its elements by mass, regardless of source.
    
- **Law of Multiple Proportions**: When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element occur in ratios of small whole numbers. For example, if $14.0\text{ g}$ of nitrogen combines with $16.0\text{ g}$ of oxygen in $\text{N}_2\text{O}_2$, it will combine with $24.0\text{ g}$ of oxygen to form $\text{N}_2\text{O}_3$ ($16.0 \times 1.5 = 24.0$).

**COAST Problem Solving Framework**
- **Collect and Organize**: Identify the problem's key concepts and define relevant terms. Restate the problem in your own words to clarify the goal. Separate relevant information from unnecessary details. Assemble necessary data, including given values, equations, definitions, and constants.
    
- **Analyze**: Connect your gathered information to the final answer. Work backward from the desired answer to determine intermediate steps. Check the units of your given values to identify which equations apply. Rearrange equations to isolate the unknown variable before plugging in numbers. Draw a sketch of the molecular model or experimental setup to visualize the problem. Estimate an order of magnitude answer to check your final calculation later.
    
- **Solve**: Manipulate equations to isolate the unknown variable. Insert your starting values and perform the calculation. Verify that your units cancel correctly. Ensure your final answer uses the correct number of significant figures.
    
- **Think About It**: Review your calculated result to determine if it makes logical sense. Compare your final answer to your initial estimate. Confirm the units and significant figures are correct. Consider how the core concept applies to daily life or other problem contexts.

# Chapter 1.3 
## Matter and Chemistry Fundamentals

- **Matter:** You define matter as everything in the universe that has mass and occupies space.
    
- **Chemistry:** You study the composition, structure, and properties of matter, along with the changes it undergoes.
    
- **Pure Substances:** You classify elements and compounds as pure substances. You cannot separate them into simpler substances using physical processes like melting or boiling. Gold and water are practical examples.
    
- **Particles:** You observe matter at the particle level as atoms, molecules, or ions. Molecules are neutral groups of bonded atoms. Ions are particles with a net positive or negative electrical charge.
    
- **Physical processes:** transformation of a sample of matter, such as a change in its physical state, that does not alter the chemical identity of any substance in the sample; also called physical change
## Physical and Chemical Properties

- **Physical Properties:** You can observe or measure these without altering the chemical identity of your sample. For example, pure gold is malleable, ductile, and melts at 1064°C.
    
- **Chemical Properties:** You observe these when elements react and transform into compounds with different chemical identities. This includes reaction rates and the specific products formed. For instance, elemental gold resists water but dissolves in specific acid mixtures.
    
- **Compound Differences:** You will notice compounds exhibit entirely different properties than their constituent elements. While hydrogen is highly flammable and oxygen supports combustion, their compound, water, extinguishes fires and exists as a liquid at room temperature.
    

## Extensive and Intensive Properties

- **Extensive Properties:** These depend on how much of the substance you have. Examples include mass, volume, length, and width.
    
- **Intensive Properties:** These remain constant regardless of your sample size. Examples include color, hardness, and density. You calculate **density** as the ratio of mass to volume using the formula $d = \frac{m}{V}$.
    

## Mixtures and Separation Techniques

- **Homogeneous Mixtures:** You will find the substances distributed uniformly in these mixtures. Scientists also call these solutions. Filtered air is a gaseous solution.
    
- **Heterogeneous Mixtures:** You will see distinct regions of different compositions because the substances do not distribute uniformly. A fruit salad or immiscible liquids like oil and water fit this category.
    
- **Distillation:** You separate components based on different boiling temperatures. You can purify seawater by evaporating the water and condensing the vapor, leaving the solid salts behind.
    
- **Filtration:** You remove solid particles suspended in liquids or gases. Hospitals use HEPA filters to pull dust and bacteria from the air.

# Chapter 1.4 
**States of Matter** You can classify matter into three distinct physical states based on volume and shape.

- **Solid:** You observe a definite volume and shape. Particles pack closely together in an ordered arrangement and lock in place. They vibrate but do not move past one another. Ice serves as a primary example.
    
- **Liquid:** You observe a definite volume but no definite shape. The liquid takes the shape of your container. Particles sit close together but lack strict order, allowing them to tumble and flow past each other.
    
- **Gas:** You observe neither a definite volume nor a definite shape. Gases expand to fill their containers entirely. Particles remain widely separated by empty space, making gases highly compressible. Water vapor is a common example.
    

**Phase Changes** You alter the physical state of a substance by adding or removing energy through heating or cooling.

- **Energy Absorbed:** You add heat to drive these transitions.
    
    - **Melting:** A solid transforms into a liquid.
        
    - **Vaporization:** A liquid transforms into a gas.
        
    - **Sublimation:** A solid transforms directly into a gas. Dry ice, or solid carbon dioxide, sublimes into gas at room temperature.
        
- **Energy Released:** You remove heat to drive these transitions.
    
    - **Condensation:** A gas transforms into a liquid. Water beads forming on a cold glass demonstrate this process.
        
    - **Freezing:** A liquid transforms into a solid.
        
    - **Deposition:** A gas transforms directly into a solid.
        

**Particulate Views** You can identify the physical state of a substance by examining its molecular structure.

- Ordered particles with little space between them represent a solid.
    
- Disordered particles with little space between them represent a liquid.
    
- Widely separated particles represent a gas.

# Chapter 1.5 
**Molecules and Chemical Formulas**

You define a molecule as a collection of atoms held together by chemical bonds. Certain pure elements, like hydrogen and oxygen, exist naturally as diatomic molecules consisting of two atoms. You use molecular formulas to show the exact number of each atom present in a single molecule. Acetone and acetic acid provide clear examples where subscripts indicate these exact quantities.

**Structural Representations**

Molecular formulas do not reveal how atoms connect. You can use structural formulas to visualize the specific chemical bonds between atoms. You can also write condensed structural formulas to save space by grouping common bonded atoms together.

**Three Dimensional Models**

Chemists use different models depending on the specific properties they study.

- **Ball and stick models:** You use balls for atoms and sticks for bonds. This method accurately displays the angles between bonds but makes atoms appear artificially far apart.
    
- **Space filling models:** You use this model to see the true three dimensional shape and arrangement of atoms. This format represents atomic overlap accurately but obscures internal bond angles.
    

**Ionic Compounds and Empirical Formulas**

You will encounter ionic compounds that do not form individual molecules. These consist of positively and negatively charged ions attracted to one another. Table salt, or sodium chloride, exists as an ordered three dimensional crystal of sodium and chloride ions. You represent these compounds using an empirical formula. This formula provides the simplest whole number ratio of the ions present in the crystal.

**Energy Basics and Kinetic Energy**

You define energy as the capacity to do work. Work occurs when you move an object over a distance. Kinetic energy represents the energy of motion. You calculate it using the equation $KE = \frac{1}{2}mu^2$, where $m$ is mass and $u$ is speed. A heavy object holds more kinetic energy than a light one moving at the exact same speed. If you double an object's speed, you quadruple its kinetic energy.

**Potential Energy and Coulomb Potential**

Potential energy is stored energy resulting from an object's position or chemical composition. When you exercise, your body converts the chemical potential energy stored in glucose into kinetic energy to move your muscles.

At the atomic level, chemical bonds and ionic attractions store potential energy. You use the Coulomb potential to model the electrostatic energy between two charged particles: $PE = \kappa \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{d}$.

When two particles carry opposite charges, their potential energy is negative. This indicates a stable, favorable attraction. As you increase the distance between them, the magnitude of their attraction decreases. Particles with the exact same charge repel each other, creating a positive potential energy.

**Heat and Conservation of Energy**

Heat is energy transferred strictly due to a difference in temperatures. Heat transfers spontaneously from a warmer object to a cooler one. The law of conservation of energy dictates that you cannot create or destroy energy. You can only convert it from one form to another.

**Kinetic Energy and States of Matter**

The particles composing all matter possess kinetic energy that scales directly with temperature. When oxygen molecules in the air absorb heat, they move faster. In solid ice, water molecules lock into position with limited kinetic energy. In liquid water, they tumble past one another with higher energy. In water vapor, they move freely with the highest kinetic energy.

**SI Units and the Metric System**

You use the Système International d’Unités (SI units) to standardize scientific measurements. The system relies on base units like the meter for length, the kilogram for mass, the kelvin for temperature, and the second for time. You derive other units from these foundations. You calculate volume in cubic meters and speed in meters per second. You apply decimal prefixes to scale units up or down. A prefix like kilo multiplies a unit by one thousand, while a prefix like milli divides it by one thousand.

**Temperature Scales**

You encounter Fahrenheit, Celsius, and Kelvin scales in measurement. Scientists primarily use Celsius and Kelvin. The Celsius scale establishes water's freezing point at 0°C and its boiling point at 100°C. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (0 K). This zero point corresponds to an absolute lack of kinetic energy in a substance. You use Kelvin for most scientific equations. You convert a Celsius temperature to Kelvin by adding 273.15.

**Exact and Inexact Values**

You generate exact values by counting distinct objects. Counting 12 eggs or 893 stair steps provides an exact number with zero uncertainty. You generate inexact values whenever you measure a continuous quantity. Weighing a marble block or measuring a room's length introduces uncertainty because all analytical methods and instruments have limitations.

**Precision and Accuracy**

You use precision to describe how consistently you can repeat a measurement. If you weigh a metal block five times and read 2.5271 g each time, your scale is highly precise. You use accuracy to describe how close your measurement sits to the true value. You verify an instrument's accuracy through calibration. You place a thermometer in boiling water to confirm it reads exactly 100.0°C.

You use significant figures to indicate your exact level of certainty in a measured value. A value of 2.5271 g contains five significant figures. You must apply specific rules to determine if zeros are significant:

- Zeros at the beginning of a number (0.0592) never count.
    
- Zeros after a decimal point and a nonzero digit (3.00) always count.
    
- Zeros trapped between significant digits (101.3) always count.
    
- Trailing zeros in a number without a decimal (96,500) remain ambiguous. You should use scientific notation to clarify your certainty.
    

**Calculations with Significant Figures**

You only round your numbers at the final step of a calculation to prevent intermediate errors. You apply the weak link principle to your final answer. Your result can only be as precise as your least precise starting value. When you multiply or divide, you limit your final answer to the exact number of significant figures as the starting value with the fewest significant figures. When you add or subtract, you limit your answer to the same number of decimal places as the starting value with the fewest decimal places.

**Dimensional Analysis and Conversion Factors**

- You use dimensional analysis to convert a measured quantity from one unit to another by multiplying it by a unit conversion factor.
    
- A conversion factor is a ratio of equivalent quantities (e.g., $0.80 \text{ kg} = 1.0 \text{ L}$). Multiplying by this factor is mathematically equivalent to multiplying by 1, changing the units without altering the actual amount.
    
- Set up your equations so the starting units cancel out, leaving only the target units: 
- Initial units × (desired units / initial units) = desired units.
    
- Always write out the units alongside your numbers. Skipping units leads to critical real-world failures, such as Air Canada Flight 143 running out of fuel due to mixing up pounds and kilograms, and the Mars Climate Orbiter crashing from a mix-up in metric and imperial thrust units.
    

**Interconverting Imperial and SI Units**

- You can chain multiple conversion factors to solve complex, multi-step problems, such as converting a drug dosage based on a patient's weight in pounds into a liquid volume in milliliters.
    
- When converting multidimensional units like area or volume, you must apply the exponent to the entire conversion factor (both the number and the unit). To convert cubic inches to cubic centimeters, you cube the entire linear relationship: $(1 \text{ in})^3 = (2.54 \text{ cm})^3 = 16.387064 \text{ cm}^3$.
    

**Temperature Conversions**

- You convert between the Kelvin and Celsius scales by adding or subtracting their zero-point difference, 273.15, because the magnitude of one degree is identical on both scales.
    
- You calculate Kelvin using the formula: $T(\text{K}) = T(\text{°C}) + 273.15$.

**Evaluating Precision and Accuracy**

- You analyze control samples containing known quantities to ensure your experimental results are accurate.
    
- You calculate the **mean** ($\bar{x}$) by summing all individual values ($x_i$) and dividing by the total number of measurements ($n$):
    
    $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum (x_i)}{n}$$
    
- You measure precision using the **standard deviation** ($s$). A smaller $s$ means your measurements cluster tightly around the mean, indicating high precision. You calculate it by squaring the deviation of each value from the mean, summing them, dividing by $n-1$, and taking the square root:
    
    $$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}}$$
    
- You report standard deviation alongside the mean using a $\pm$ sign to show the uncertainty boundaries, such as $0.6820 \pm 0.0094$ mg/dL.
    

**Confidence Intervals**

- You calculate a **confidence interval** to determine if your results are quantitatively accurate. This interval provides a range that likely contains the true mean value ($\mu$).
    
- You use the $t$-distribution and choose a confidence level, typically 95%, meaning there is a 95% chance the calculated range contains the true mean:
    
    $$\mu = \bar{x} \pm \frac{ts}{\sqrt{n}}$$
    
- If the known actual value of your control sample falls within this calculated range, you consider your analysis accurate.
    

**Normal Distributions and Outliers**

- Random variability in large datasets produces a normal distribution shaped like a bell curve. In a normal distribution, exactly 68% of your values fall within one standard deviation of the mean.
    
- An **outlier** is an individual value sitting significantly further from the mean than the rest of your data. You cannot discard an unexpected value simply because it looks different or inconvenient.
    
- You apply **Grubbs' test** to mathematically justify removing a suspect outlier. You divide the absolute difference between the suspect value ($x_i$) and the mean ($\bar{x}$) by the standard deviation ($s$) to find $Z$:
    
    $$Z = \frac{\vert{}x_i - \bar{x}\vert{}}{s}$$
    
- If your calculated $Z$ is greater than the reference $Z$ value for your specific sample size and confidence level, you can discard the outlier. You can only use Grubbs' test one single time per data set to remove a maximum of one outlier.
