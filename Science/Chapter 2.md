# Chapter 2.1 
## The Discovery of the Electron

- **Cathode-Ray Tube Experiment (1897):** J.J. Thomson passed electricity through a glass vacuum tube. He observed a beam that traveled from the negative cathode to the positive anode.
    
- **Key Findings:** Magnetic and electric fields deflected the beam. This proved the rays were negatively charged particles. Thomson calculated their mass-to-charge ratio and identified them as fundamental particles, now known as electrons.
    
- **Millikan's Oil-Drop Experiment (1912):** Robert Millikan sprayed oil drops into a chamber and used X-rays to ionize the air. The oil drops absorbed free electrons as they fell.
    
- **Key Findings:** By adjusting a vertical electric field, Millikan altered the fall rate of the charged drops. He determined the exact charge of a single electron is $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C and calculated its mass as $9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
    

## The Gold-Foil Experiment and the Nucleus

- **Early Theory:** Scientists initially believed atoms were solid spheres of positive charge with scattered electrons.
    
- **Rutherford's Experiment (1909):** Ernest Rutherford, Hans Geiger, and Ernest Marsden fired positively charged alpha particles at a thin piece of gold foil.
    
- **Observations:** Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil. A tiny fraction deflected at sharp angles, and a few bounced directly backward toward the source.
    
- **The Nuclear Model:** The deflections proved the atom is mostly empty space. A gold atom contains a tiny, dense, positively charged core called the nucleus. The nucleus is roughly 10,000 times smaller than the total diameter of the atom.
    

## Protons and Neutrons

- **Protons:** Rutherford observed that bombarding elements with alpha particles produced hydrogen nuclei. He identified these positive particles as protons and concluded they exist in all atomic nuclei.
    
- **Neutrons:** Rutherford predicted another particle existed to account for the total mass of the nucleus. James Chadwick confirmed the existence of the free neutron in 1932. Neutrons have no electrical charge but carry nearly the same mass as a proton.

# Chapter 2.2 
**Thomson and Aston's Experiment**

- J.J. Thomson and Francis W. Aston (1912) modified cathode-ray tubes to study beams of positively charged particles.
    
- They passed an electric current through a tube containing neon gas within a magnetic field.
    
- The neon atoms lost electrons and formed positively charged $Ne^+$ ions.
    
- These ions separated into two distinct patches on a photographic plate, proving that the $Ne^+$ ions had two different masses (20 u and 22 u).
    
- The lighter 20 u ions produced a brighter patch, indicating they were significantly more abundant (about 90% of the total).
    

**Isotopes and Nuclides**

- **Isotopes:** Atoms of the same element that contain the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
    
- **Nuclide:** Any atom of any element that has a specific number of neutrons in its nucleus.
    
- Thomson and Aston's findings required scientists to update John Dalton’s definition of an element. Instead of being matter composed of atoms with identical mass, an element is now defined as matter composed of atoms that all share the same number of protons.
    

**Atomic Number and Mass Number**

- **Atomic Number ($Z$):** The exact number of protons in a nucleus. This value alone identifies the specific element.
    
- **Mass Number ($A$):** The total combined number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) in an atom's nucleus.
    
- You can determine the number of neutrons ($n$) by subtracting the atomic number from the mass number: $n = A - Z$.
    

**Nuclide Symbols**

- The standard notation for a specific nuclide is $_{Z}^{A}X$.
    
    - $X$ = the element's one- or two-letter symbol.
        
    - $Z$ = the atomic number (protons).
        
    - $A$ = the mass number (protons + neutrons).
        
- Because $Z$ and $X$ provide redundant information, you can drop the atomic number subscript and write the symbol simply as $^{A}X$ (e.g., $^{16}O$ or $^{20}Ne$).
    
- You write the names of these nuclides by combining the element name and the mass number (e.g., oxygen-16, neon-20).
    

**Ionic Nuclides**

- When neutral atoms gain or lose electrons, they become charged ions. You indicate this charge by adding a superscript $Q$ to the symbol: $_{Z}^{A}X^{Q}$.
    
- $Q$ represents the net positive or negative charge.
    
- You calculate the charge by subtracting electrons ($e$) from protons ($p$): $Q = p - e$.
    
- **Example:** The most common oxygen ion ($Z = 8$) has a $2-$ charge. This means it has 8 protons and $8 + 2 = 10$ electrons. Its symbol is $^{16}O^{2-}$.

**You can find an element's group by looking at the numbers across the very top of the columns on the periodic table. The table has 18 columns, and you can use these numbers to quickly determine how many valence electrons the main elements have.**

- **Columns 1 and 2:** The group number equals the number of valence electrons. Elements in column 1 have 1 valence electron, and elements in column 2 have 2.
    
- **Columns 13 through 18:** Subtract 10 from the group number to find the valence electrons. For example, elements in column 13 have 3 valence electrons, and elements in column 17 have 7.
    
- **Columns 3 through 12 (Transition Metals):** Skip these columns for this rule, as their electron behaviors do not follow the same simple pattern.
