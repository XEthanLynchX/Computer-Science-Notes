### Hook

What if the most important money decision you'll ever make happens before you've even made any real money?

### My story

From 16 to 18, I worked for $15 an hour. That might be where you are right now, or where you were not long ago.

Like most teenagers, the second I got my check, I was already thinking about what to spend it on.

One of the dumbest purchases I ever made was a VR headset. I spent almost $500 on it, plus a few hundred more on games. I told myself I worked hard and earned it, so I had the right to spend it. And I did, for about two weeks. Then I played it once a week. Then not at all.

I was working all the time and I was always broke. But I lived at home, so being broke didn't really matter. My only real expense was going out to eat with friends.

Then reality hit, and it hit hard.

My home life wasn't great to begin with. My uncle moved back in because he was about to have a baby. What I quickly found out was that he had a serious drinking problem.

One day I did some landscaping work at a friend's house for a couple hundred bucks. That was a lot of money to me back then, and it was hard work. I got home exhausted and passed out in bed.

My uncle had moved in three days earlier. I woke up to him yelling at someone upstairs. When I got to the top of the stairs, I saw him yelling at my mom and my aunt. I yelled, "Hey!" He turned around and grabbed me by the neck.

We fought our way down the hallway and fell to the ground. I got up and locked myself in the bathroom. I was crying, yelling "What is wrong with you?" I heard him go back down the hall and keep yelling at them. I ran downstairs to my room and called the police.

When they showed up, I told them my side and he told them his. They also talked to my mom and aunt, who saw the whole thing. When the officer came back, I could tell from his face it was bad news.

"Unfortunately, there's nothing we can do."

My uncle said I attacked him. My mom and aunt said they didn't see what happened.

So I had a choice. Stay and risk something worse, or leave. That night I packed everything I owned and left.

I slept in my car for a few days. Then I called my other aunt and uncle, and thankfully they let me stay with them for a while.

### Why I'm telling you this

I'm not sharing this to scare you. I'm sharing it because your whole life can flip upside down overnight.

If I'd known that spending every dollar I made would leave me that vulnerable, I wouldn't have done it.

If you're on this side of YouTube, that might sound obvious. It wasn't obvious to 18-year-old me.

Today, instead of living paycheck to paycheck, I have over $15,000 saved. That might not sound like a lot, but only 47% of Americans could cover a surprise $1,000 expense, and about a third of Gen Z has no emergency savings at all.

I built that cushion by budgeting and spending on purpose. And it didn't start until after I moved out of my aunt and uncle's place, when I was so broke I couldn't even afford a haircut.

So here's the 5-step plan I used, and one you can use for the rest of your life.

### Step 1: Know your income vs. expenses

First I had to figure out my must-pay expenses every month: rent, food, gas, insurance, loan payments.

Then I cut the extras. Subscriptions, eating out, money I spent on stuff I liked to do.

I compared what was left to my income, and at first I could only save about $300 a month.

I won't lie, it sucked. But I kept reminding myself what I was saving for. Right now, I wanted security, so I didn't have to stress about where rent was coming from. Long term, I wanted a better life, and a better life for my future kids, so they never have to deal with what I did.

### Step 2: Set goals

What's the point of budgeting? It gives your money a plan, so you can pay your bills, stay out of debt, and save for what actually matters to you.

For me, that was feeling secure and being ready for the next surprise. For you, it might be a car, a house, or moving out. Everyone's different. Either way, having money saved will never hurt you.

So I set goals: $1,000. Then $3,000. Then $5,000. Then $10,000.

Hitting each one feels amazing. But you quickly realize it's never really "enough," and that's what keeps you going.

### Step 3: Turn your goals into steps

The easiest way to hit a goal is to work backward.

Take your goal, divide it by the number of months you want to reach it, and that's your monthly number. If you want $3,000 in a year, that's $250 a month. That's roughly $60 a week, or skipping eating out a couple of times.

Then make it automatic. The day your paycheck hits, move your savings into a separate account before you touch anything else. People call this "paying yourself first." If you never see that money in your checking account, you won't miss it.

### Step 5: Put your money to work

Once your emergency fund is set, saving alone isn't enough. Money sitting in a checking account loses value to inflation. So the next step is investing, and this is where starting young pays off.

Picture two people who each invest $200 a month, assuming an average 7% yearly return.

Person A starts at 18, invests until 30, and then stops forever. They put in $28,800.

Person B starts at 30 and invests until 60. They put in $72,000.

At 60, Person A has over $340,000. Person B has about $245,000.

Person A put in less than half the money and still came out way ahead, because their money had 12 extra years to grow. That's compounding.

The time you waste now isn't just time. It's what your money could have made for you later.
